# Wyckoff — How I Trade and Invest in Stocks and Bonds (1924) — notes (read 2026-11-11, in chat)
## Chunk p.2–p.36 (read)
- p.17–18: paper-trade study for 2–3 years (not months) before risking money; author studied 8 yrs before investing, 6 more before trading. → agrees with practice principle / R-048 (D, own history).
- p.18: prices move on three factors beyond value: manipulation, technical conditions, trend of the market. p.19: trend is the most important technical factor; overbought/oversold condition decides the direction of the NEXT swing. → principle D (no measurable definition given yet).
- p.22–23: traders tend to take small profits and big losses; bucket-shop owner profited by taking the other side. → agrees with loss-aversion principle (C anecdote).
- p.24: the one persistently successful trader he saw put a 2-point stop on every order, traded active stocks both ways, took slightly more than 2 points. Panic of 1893 (GE 114→20) shows the cost of no stop. → C example; agrees with "set the exit before entering" principle; possible agree for R-009-type (fixed stop on every order).
- p.25: sudden wealth → over-extension and big losses; build up slowly. → principle D.
- p.26–27: steady buyers of reorganized/receivership railroad stocks and bonds at low prices made the big gains in the 1896 bull market. → C example; principle (buy sound reorganized securities when depressed) — no measurable trigger.
- p.29: manipulation aims to make the public buy, sell or keep out; manipulators do the opposite. → principle D.
- p.30–31: Dow's three simultaneous movements: long trend (years), 30–60 day swings, small swings (1–several days). → principle D (cite of Dow, no data).
- p.35–36: most traders ignorant, mentally lazy, want tips ("lean, not learn"); they do the opposite of large experienced operators because they judge by surface conditions. → principle D.
## Chunk p.37–p.63 (read)
- p.38: charts are the record of many minds' impressions; study them to understand the reasoning behind moves, not to follow blindly. → principle D.
- p.40: author got better results trading 5/10/20-point swings than daily scalps; "the real money" in 30–60 day swings where accumulation/distribution is visible in the preparatory stage. → principle C (own experience, no numbers).
- p.41–42: a campaign has 3 stages: accumulation (weeks–months), mark-up (news/aggressive buying), distribution; operators often overshoot and distribute on the way down (accumulate 50–60, target average 80, run to 100, sell down to 70). → principle D (illustration only).
- p.42–43: impatience was his biggest fault; brokers sometimes made more in commissions than he made. → principle (costs/overtrading) agrees.
- p.44: trades with only 5–10% of his liquid capital; profits go into income securities ("I trade so that I may invest"). → principle C (own practice; Cyrus's capital is the input, no data source).
- p.45–46: U.S. Steel campaign method: when a sharp move is expected, buy 300 sh with a 3-point stop; add 100 sh every 2 points in his favour, each with its own 3-point stop; stop adding after ~10 points (800 sh); then take profit on further advance or raise the stop to lock in profit. Three campaigns, ≤$3,000 margin each, ~$20,000 net that year. → RULE candidate: pyramid only into winners, fixed add interval, each add with its own stop, cap the adds. C (own trades). Compatible with R-022 (never add to losers).
- p.47: second-hand: a successful speculator buys 500, adds every point up, sells at once if it falls 2–3 points (first judgment wrong). → agrees with the pyramid rule (C, hearsay).
- p.47–49: merchants/real-estate habits (buy and wait) fail in trading; trader must short as readily as buy. → agrees with MetaStock short principle (D).
- p.50 SIX RULES: (1) the main factor is the trend — working with it gives "three or four times" the chances; in a downtrend a long only exits on a rally → agrees R-026 trend-direction (author claims 3–4×, D). (2) risk should almost always be limited: 2- or 3-point stop behind every trading position (Harriman: 3/8–1 point) → agrees "set the exit before entering" principle; agrees R-009 spirit.
- p.51: (3) anticipated profit at least 3–4× the risk; protect profits by raising stops or selling half to cut the cost of the rest → agrees R-010 (stricter: 3–4×) and R-029-ish half-sale idea. (4) deal freely on both sides, or be an investor buying sound stocks below intrinsic value in panics → principle. (5) deal in active stocks — a stock must move; don't tie money up in sluggish ones → principle D (no threshold). 
- p.52: (6) make trading a business or don't trade; else be an intelligent investor → principle.
- p.55: three lines of defence: solid income investments, income-and-profit securities, a front line of speculation → principle D.
- p.55–63: equipment-trust bonds, short-term notes, railroad bonds bought "too low" in Dec 1919, buying twice what you keep on bank credit → bonds and leverage, OUT OF DESK SCOPE; not carried. p.58 Harriman: "I want something that will grow" → principle D.
## Chunk p.64–p.91 (read)
- p.64–67: bank stocks held in a separate trust account; reinvest every dividend, right and stock distribution, never spend them; author estimates >12%/yr on leading issues, doubling in 6–7 yrs (own estimate, no data). → principle C/D (compounding by reinvesting distributions). Buying ahead of income on trust-company credit: leverage, not carried.
- p.71: keeps holdings marketable so he can act on a threatened panic or bargain. → principle D (keep liquid reserve for panics). Bonds selling below intrinsic value (p.71–72): bonds out of scope.
- p.72–74: convertible bonds (out of scope); but: selling part of a holding at a profit marks down the cost of the rest — "every investor should remember" → principle D.
- p.76: chain-store and mail-order companies preferred because they reinvest more earnings than other groups; Sears pays a stock dividend every 3–4 years. → principle D (favour companies that retain and reinvest earnings; no threshold).
- p.77–78: bought DL&W for buried equity: profit-and-loss surplus $57.2M vs $42.3M stock outstanding (1918), heavy reinvestment in the property; bought at ~180 on a threatened coal strike vs 1919 high 217, low since 1906 of 160. → C example.
- p.78: "securities more often reach their low point when some danger or disaster is threatened, than upon the actual occurrence" — fearful holders have sold by then; after the event the price rallies on short covering or new investment demand. → principle D (Wall Street history asserted, no data).
- p.79–80: high-priced stocks with real earnings/dividends are cheaper than low-priced ones earning 1–2%; a stock paying 5–8% is worth $60–100, paying 1% worth $12–20, a non-payer $0–12 — "everything above that is hope capitalized." → principle D; in tension with Heitkoetter's low-priced-stock principle; agrees with R-027's low-price exclusion.
- p.81: favour companies that keep putting money back into the property; watch for a change in policy or control; persistent liquidation in the stock was the warning sign at New Haven. → principle C (one case).
- p.81–82: spread money and "watch all of the baskets"; never get married to a security; review holdings periodically against alternatives. → principle D; agrees with Hammond diversification principle.
- p.82–83: a stockholder is a partner: judge management by its past record, stay sceptical until it proves it works for shareholders. → principle D.
- p.84–87: speculative/mining ventures: only put in money you are prepared to lose entirely; check who else is invested and their record, financing, the engineer, market for the product, ore life and profit per share; pay for an independent second expert. → principle D (due-diligence checklist for speculative early-stage companies).
- p.88–91: Magma Copper (C, own trade): "I always lay more stress on the action of the market than on what anybody says"; bought 200, added 1,000 as it pushed up through 21–23 ("I always like to buy something that is hard to purchase"); ran to 69 in 3 weeks; he held for the mine's value, planning to sell when the controlling holder starts to distribute; insiders held ~220,000 of 240,000 shares. → principle C (act on price action not tips; buy strength that is hard to buy; exit when the controlling holder distributes).
## Chunk p.92–p.121 (read)
- p.92–93: investigate thoroughly, then hold "like grim death" until something definite changes the premise; never trade on hindsight. Waiting for a big profit is hard; assemble the facts at entry and re-check them all the way. → principle D/C.
- p.97: prefer companies selling necessities (fuel, food, steel, machinery) with continual demand (Rockefeller holdings). → principle D.
- p.98–101: SIX-STEP INVESTMENT CHECKLIST, in this order: (1) long trend of the market; (2) nature and tendency of the industry; (3) trend of the company's affairs (improving or not); (4) character and reputation of management; (5) financial position and earning power; (6) position in the intermediate 30–60 day swings. Step 4 has no data source → the checklist is a PRINCIPLE (D). 
- p.99–100: "the long trend of the market … is the compass"; won't make an investment unless the long trend is upward; buying in a bear market means a long carry, maybe through receivership; even weak stocks rise in a bull market. → agrees R-050 (index trend filter), D. Market action discounts business 6–12 months ahead → principle D.
- p.102–104: industries are linked (autos → tires, steel, oil); some groups rise while the market falls; choose the industries likely to do best by elimination. → agrees R-069 (strongest sectors) / R-051 spirit, D.
- p.105–106: for speculative purchases technical position weighs as much as fundamentals; prefers a stock that ran 100→150, reacted to 110 on bear pressure with fundamentals unchanged and now shows "strong resistance to pressure", over one still being distributed near its top after a 40–50 point markup with heavy activity. → principle D (no measurable test of "resistance").
- p.106–107: Dow: "the public rarely sees values until they are pointed out"; rising prices themselves suggest higher prices to the public; pools accumulate without lifting price, then bid it up to draw the public in, and depress stocks to shake them out. → principle D.
- p.108: large interests keep scale orders on both sides (buy declines, sell rallies) and so always have cash for the next decline. → principle D.
- p.108–109: technical position: weak on the long side when many outside speculators hold the stock for a profit and new buying is exhausted — it must fall whatever the fundamentals; strong when shorts have sold all they can and demand overcomes supply: shorts are future buyers, "an element of strength". → principle D (short interest is observable, but "buying power exhausted" isn't defined).
- p.112: profit-taking: not by points or % gained, but hold until the insiders begin to sell. → principle C (he judged insider selling from the stock's action, p.121; Form 4/144 data could make it testable today — desk note, not the book's).
- p.113–121: Graphophone (C, own trade): new policy of $2.50 quarterly cash + 1/20 share stock dividend = ~$37/share/yr at 135 → he figured fair value 200–300 and bought 40 sh at ~164 average; it ran to 500 pre-split; the stock held 43–46 while the rest of the market was weak (relative strength), then broke out on 50,000–75,000 shares a day; he planned to sell part on a decline back to 70 when "certain indications" and the stock's action suggested insiders selling. → principle C (value a new payout policy by total cash + stock dividend at market; relative strength against a weak market before the move).
## Chunk p.122–p.159 (read)
- p.122–123: when insiders sold he sold 263 of 463 shares at 70, recovering the original $6,575 plus $12,080 cash and keeping 200 shares at below-zero cost; selling near the top funds re-buying lower. → principle C (sell enough on strength to recover the cost; hold the rest free).
- p.123: the "inside information" said +15 points and was wrong (it rose hundreds); he acted on public facts confirmed at the company's office. → principle C (tips are unreliable; act on verifiable public facts).
- p.127–129: nobody is right continuously; author reports Keene said 6 right out of 10 was doing well; Keene and Harriman went through bad periods; a floor trader closes any position once its loss bothers him ($20–25k). → principle D (expect a share of losses; success = profits exceed losses).
- p.130: he prefers taking losses to holding positions that become "Hopes" or "Faint Hopes". → agrees loss-aversion principle.
- p.130–131: LIVERMORE TIME STOP (as described to the author): unless a trade (long or short) shows a profit within 2–3 days, close it — "whenever I find myself hoping that a trade will come out all right, I get out"; keeps capital circulating. Plus a mental price stop a few points away: a time stop and a price stop together. → RULE candidate: time stop 2–3 days on short-term trades. D (second-hand practice, no results). Conflict with R-044's 20-day time limit → decided by horizon/strategy.
- p.131–132: author traces most of his principal losses to not placing a stop when the trade was made (losses of 5–10 points that could have been 2–3). → agrees "set the exit before entering" principle, C (own losses).
- p.132–133: best results: wait for an important turning point, limit risk, trade the 10–20 point swings; very active in-and-out trading mostly pays the broker (commissions, taxes) — floor members have an edge. → agrees costs principle (Salvon loc.24), C.
- p.133: worthwhile changes rarely happen within one session; "the day to buy is not the day to sell" (C. Hayden). → principle D; supports the desk's no-intraday stance.
- p.133–135: his best market judgment came from one hour a day, no news ticker, judging only from market action; an out-of-town trader needs only each watched stock's high, low and last for the day — not volume or news — to place next morning's orders. → principle C (daily bars are enough; avoid news noise).
- p.138: [Numeric table omitted] — oil consumption vs production graph referenced on p.140–141 → check table p.138.
- p.139–146: industry selection by supply/demand: oil chosen because consumption ran ahead of production for 2 years (graph, p.138) and Standard Oil NJ forecast 675M bbl demand by 1925 vs 376M produced in 1920; producers rather than refiners hold the winning cards. "Market movements, when correctly forecasted, pay more dollars than dividends." → principle C/D (favour industries where demand outruns supply; one forecast).
- p.148–153 AVERAGING DOWN: price falling doesn't make a stock cheaper — value can shrink faster than price; know WHY it fell before adding. His case: bought ~45; it fell to 30 (the underwriting price; promoters walked away); he waited until ~15, confirmed with officers that the company had cut its debt and raised earning power while the price fell, then bought an equal amount, sold it +10 points, re-bought ~12, sold 16, finally sold at 38 — about even over 2–3 years. Most averagers start too soon or lack capital for a fall 2–3× what they expect (friend bought Union Pacific 185/160/135 after 219, wiped out at 116). Author claims 85–90% of business, investment and speculative failures come from over-trading or lack of capital. → RULE candidate: average down only when the fundamentals improved while the price fell, with reserve capital, and sell the added lot on rallies to cut cost. C (one own case, ended about even). CONFLICT with R-022 (never add to a loser) → decided by whether reported debt fell and earnings rose since purchase.
- p.153–155: periodically take stock of your own results; if the last months or years were unprofitable, suspend operations until you find the cause, then practise on paper or with 10-share lots until fixed; study misfortunes more than triumphs; be neither a chronic bull nor bear. → agrees MetaStock p.13 (paper-trade after a losing streak) and journaling principle; relates to R-049 circuit breaker (D).
## Chunk p.160–p.187 (read)
- p.160: when a situation is unclear, write all the facts down as favourable/unfavourable before deciding. → principle D (agrees with the written-plan/journal principle).
- p.161–162: primary and ultimate object is safe, profitable investment; trading profits go into income securities that can grow, with income reinvested. → principle D (already noted p.44).
- p.162–163: if you are not suited to trading, prove it to yourself and stop; his friend saw his bond savings creeping into the margin account and quit. → principle C: if trading keeps drawing down your invested capital, stop and reassess.
- p.164–166: switch out of mediocre holdings even at a loss when another will work harder; e.g. swap a preferred covering its dividend 1.5× for one covering it 3–4× at the same price. Use only a small part of capital for trading; someone who can't succeed with a small stake will lose a large one. → principle D (preferreds/bonds are out of desk scope; the switching idea is general).
- p.166–168: very few people are fitted to be traders; many can be successful investors; a few years should show which you are. → principle D.
- p.168–170: foresight is the essence of speculation; without it you are gambling (Morgan, Harriman). → principle D.
- p.170–172: depend on your own judgment, not tips or inside connections — even a true inside tip leads to holding too long or plunging afterwards; "the crowd is generally wrong". → principle D; agrees Regan loc.21 (lean against the crowd).
- p.173–175: experience of several panics lets you exploit them, provided you have put yourself in a position to buy at the lows beforehand. → principle D (keep a reserve; agrees p.71 note).
- p.178: chart of 10-year averages (figure, not a numeric table).
- p.179–183: get a good start; study before operating; never put yourself where your capital can be wiped out; lack of capital and over-trading (too heavily committed one way) cause most misfortunes. → principle D (agrees with existing risk principles).
- p.184–185: public cross-section: little interest when prices are low; buying grows with the extent and speed of an advance until the public is "95% bullish" and loaded at the top (author's estimate); in a panic they sell or are sold out to bargain hunters and those who sold high; bear markets are swift and severe — two years of paper profits can vanish in 30–60 days. → principle D (author claims 95%).
- p.186–187: opportunities keep coming and most aren't as good as they look — the best are worth waiting for; spend years of study before operating; build capital and knowledge slowly. → principle D.
