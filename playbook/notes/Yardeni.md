# Notes — Yardeni, Stock Valuation Models (Topical Study #58, 2003) — read 2026-11-09 in chat. Market-level (S&P 500) valuation vs bonds, not single stocks.
p.2      table page (omitted) — check table p.2 if a rule leans on it.
p.3      Valuation = relative (stocks vs bonds); uses 12-month forward consensus operating EPS, weekly/monthly; trailing-earnings models monitored but less useful. Valuation is judgment, not precise science.
p.4      Models explain the past; outliers carry the biggest risk/reward. 1999–2000 every model flagged overvaluation; some strategists re-engineered models to stay bullish (warning against fitting the model to the view).
p.5      SVM-1 showed record 49% undervaluation Oct 2002; author nonetheless CUT equity exposure (Jun–Jul 2002) over earnings-quality fears after an accounting fraud — model is one input (C, author's own allocation). Model breaks in deflation: very low bond yields imply implausibly high fair P/Es.
p.6–7    SVM-1 = Fed model: 10y Treasury yield vs S&P 500 forward earnings yield (12m forward consensus EPS / index). Forward EPS = time-weighted blend of current-year and next-year consensus (weights shift 1/12 per month) (p.7).
p.8      Fair value when forward earnings yield = 10y yield (avg spread 26 bp since 1979); fair price = E / 10y yield; fair P/E = 1 / 10y yield. Over/undervaluation = index / fair price − 1. Overvaluation corrects via lower rates, higher E, or lower prices.
p.8–9    Episode list 1979–2002 (case history). p.9: cites Ned Davis Research — since 1980, >5% undervalued → avg next-12m S&P gain 31.7%; >15% overvalued → avg −8.7%. Outside data, method not shown → grade B.
p.10–12  SVM-2: earnings yield = 10y + A-rated corporate spread − d × long-term consensus growth (LTEG). Implied d averaged 0.13 since 1985, 0.23 at the 1999–2000 peak, ~0.05 end-2002. High d = investors leaning on rosy long-term forecasts. Inputs (A-rated spread, S&P LTEG) have no connected data source → principle.
p.13     Analysts' long-term growth consensus is biased upward: aggregate profits can't outgrow GDP (~7%/yr historically) for long, yet consensus ran 10.8–12.1% (1985–95) and peaked 18.7% (Aug 2000). (principle, B — Thomson consensus history)
p.14     Reverse the model: market-implied forward EPS = index × 10y yield; compare with consensus to see how much of an earnings cut is already priced (principle). Model correlates for US/Canada/UK/Germany/France; fails for Japan (deflation, sub-1.5% yields).
p.15     Dividends mattered more before 1982; buybacks (SEC safe harbour 1982) shifted focus to earnings; payout ratios fell from ~55% to ~35%.
p.16–17  Earnings are easier to dress up than cash dividends; dividends discipline accounting (cash must be earned to be paid). Author's tax-policy argument — not a rule.
p.17     SVM models are NOT timing tools: over/undervaluation can extend; author says overvalued → below-average returns over the next 12–24 months, undervalued → above-average (author claim). Gordon-model family. Tobin's q: q<1 = cheaper to buy than build, but replacement-cost data make it empirically weak for the index (p.17–18).
p.18     P/E vs its own historical average ("reversion to the mean") worth tracking but ignores rates/inflation/technology; Shiller cited.
p.19     Use several disciplines; Michigan consumer expectations and "news heard" series are good at CONFIRMING major market bottoms — most depressed at troughs (charts p.29, outside data, no test stats → B, principle: no threshold given). Technical indicators as a supplement.
p.20     Analysts are too optimistic about any single year's earnings; market discounts the time-weighted forward blend.
