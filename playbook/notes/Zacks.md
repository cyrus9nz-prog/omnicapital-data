# Zacks — The Little Book of Stock Market Profits (Wiley, ©2012; catalog says 2011) — notes (read 2026-11-11, in chat)
Grading note: the book summarises academic studies (from the Handbook of Equity Market Anomalies, 650+ papers, loc.5) and Zacks database tests. A needs defined rules + sample size + period on the page; most passages give rules and period but not sample size or full method → B unless the page shows all three.
## Chunk loc.1–loc.12 (read)
- loc.1–3: contents, series list, copyright (2012 Wiley), disclaimer.
- loc.3–4: author claims US stocks have returned about 6% a year over T-bills since WWII; long-term buy and hold; day trading "far too volatile and risky" for wealth. → principle D (no source shown).
- loc.4–6: book's method: synthesises 650+ research papers (Handbook of Equity Market Anomalies, 2011) plus his portfolio-management experience; anomalies have long stretches of under- and outperformance, so patience is required. → principle (process): judge an anomaly over a full cycle; B (cites research).
- loc.6: forecasts the next decade's returns will beat the prior decade's (written 2011) → forecast, not carried as evidence.
- loc.6–8: analyst reports: EPS estimates for two fiscal years, 5-year growth, rating, 1-year target; retail investors follow the rating blindly — wrong way to use it.
- loc.8: summary: "focus on recent recommendation changes from analysts with good track records in small-cap stocks". → RULE candidate.
- loc.9: research shows: (1) recommendation CHANGES usable profitably; (2) transaction costs can sharply cut returns; (3) changes work better in small caps; (4) combine with other criteria; (5) some analysts move prices more — track their historical accuracy.
- loc.9: Zacks test, 1990–2010, quarterly: top vs bottom 10% by recommendation LEVEL → top beat bottom in 14 of 21 years, worked 1990–97 then fell apart.
- loc.10: same test sorted on recommendation CHANGES over the last month → upgrades beat downgrades in 19 of 21 years. Excess returns rise when rebalancing moves from monthly to weekly to daily; returns volatile year to year and cost-dependent; run through a full cycle (years). → RULE (growth/momentum), B (Zacks database, period and rules given, universe size and return magnitudes not shown).
- loc.10–11: transaction costs: commissions, bid/ask spreads, price impact, liquidity costs; one study: buying best-recommended stocks = 9.4% annual abnormal return before costs, −3.1% after → principle B (agrees cost principles); recommendation-level strategies have lower risk-adjusted returns than other anomalies.
- loc.12: excess returns concentrate in small firms (fewer analysts, less efficient, higher costs deter institutions); true of most anomalies; key question is whether they survive costs. → part of the revisions RULE / principle B.
- loc.12: combining positive recommendations with high price momentum, attractive valuation multiples and high earnings quality raised excess returns (one recent study) and cuts turnover (valuation stays attractive for quarters; upgrades are fleeting). Analyst optimism rises with price and earnings momentum; favourable ratings often come with less favourable valuations. → principle B.

## Chunk loc.26–loc.38 (read)

- loc.26–29: sell-side vs buy-side; value = potential cash flows (dividends) driven by earnings; institutional valuation models discount projected earnings, so higher estimates → higher fair value → buying; sell-side estimates feed buy-side models. → principle D (mechanism argument).
- loc.29–30: recommendations are biased (analysts avoid sells, love their industry); earnings estimates are "tethered to reality" by quarterly reports, though too optimistic on average (more down than up revisions over years) and swayed by accrual earnings. → principle B.
- loc.30–31: RULE: buy stocks receiving upward EPS estimate revisions from multiple analysts; avoid/sell those receiving downward revisions from multiple analysts; focus on the consensus, not one analyst. Revisions are serially correlated and the market underreacts. Research: long top 5% / short bottom 5% by revisions ≈ 10% a year gross of costs; positive, statistically significant excess returns in 1970s, 80s and 90s studies. → growth/momentum RULE, B.
- loc.32: revisions work better in smaller companies and those with fewer analysts; a revision AWAY from the consensus (e.g. $1.05 consensus → $1.10) predicts more than one TOWARD it ($1.00 → $1.05); bolder revisions (as % of consensus) predict more; much of the excess return arrives around later revisions or the next reported earnings (investors wait for confirmation). → conditions on the revisions RULE, B.
- loc.33–37: determinants of revisions (Serafeim, Handbook): price moves 11%, earnings announcements 13%, other analysts' revisions 15% (herding), deviation from consensus 12%, management guidance 3%, company-specific 46% (Exhibit 3.1, table on loc.37, read as text). Analysts revise up after price rises, after announcements/meetings, in the direction of dividend changes; analysts herd and creep incrementally; the consensus is only slightly better than a trend-line extrapolation of past earnings. → principle B.
- loc.37: international: revisions underreaction strongest in Netherlands, UK, Germany; also France, Canada; weaker in Japan and Switzerland. Larger revisions better; transaction costs reduce returns. → agrees revisions RULE (B).
- loc.38: momentum defined: rising stocks keep rising, falling keep falling; fundamental vs technical investors; most investors look at a chart before buying.
## Chunk loc.39–loc.51 (read)
- loc.39–40: testing chart patterns is a needle in a haystack (many strategies look good by chance; analysts disagree on what a signal is); Yale experiment: technical analysts found confident patterns in computer-generated random charts. → principle B (agrees Piard loc.69 caution).
- loc.41: research (autocorrelation) finds two stable patterns: short/medium-term momentum (a quarter or two) and long-term reversal (3–5 years). Hedged momentum portfolios (long top decile, short bottom decile) ≈ 12% a year gross of costs (~1% a month), positive after risk controls.
- loc.42: momentum crashed around 2000, giving back about a decade of gains within a few years; recovered after ~5 years. Best short-term variant: "12/3" — sort by past 12-month return, hold 3 months; lagging the sort by one week helps slightly. → RULE (growth/momentum), B.
- loc.43: "6/6": a 2001 study, top vs bottom decile by 6-month return held 6 months → +1.39% a month; most of it from the winners (long side). Buying stocks near their 52-week highs, or well above their 120- or 240-day moving average, does as well or better; 52-week-high slightly better than 6/6. → same RULE; agrees R-025/R-050 spirit (MA), B.
- loc.43–44: "36/36": past 36-month losers beat past 36-month winners over the next 36 months (long-term reversal; mean reversion). → RULE candidate (value, long-term): favour multi-year losers for multi-year holds; avoid multi-year winners. B. CONFLICT with the 12/3 momentum rule — decided by horizon (quarter vs years).
- loc.44–45: momentum strongest in mid caps (inverted U across size deciles; 6/6 with 30% tails, Jan 1980–Dec 1996), absent in the smallest and largest; stronger with lower analyst coverage. Works in most of 39 non-US markets with low cross-market correlation; emerging markets too. Profits hold after risk adjustment. → conditions on the RULE (B). Agrees Piard loc.36 (20-day momentum failed on large caps).
- loc.46–47: individuals hold losers too long and sell winners too soon (loss aversion ~2:1); 1983–2002 transaction data: small traders under-react; stocks with more individual ownership, lower volume, lower institutional ownership and lower analyst coverage show stronger momentum. → conditions (B); agrees loss-aversion principle.
- loc.47–48: over ~70 years momentum earned excess returns only in expansions; in recessions momentum returns turn negative and low-momentum stocks beat high over the next three months (business-cycle dating is imprecise). → INVALIDATED BY recession (B). Author: look for short-term momentum, mid caps traded mainly by individuals, hold about a quarter, select by 52-week high or moving average; beware reversals that wipe out years in months.
- loc.48–49: insiders (directors, officers, 10% owners) may trade on their own view if they report; piggyback: insiders buying → buy, insiders selling → avoid; studies show excess returns over four decades; 3,700 takeover targets: insider sales fell more than purchases in the 6 months before the deal.
- loc.50: Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) cut reporting to two days, filed electronically; strategy returns may have changed since. A study: insiders earned about 6% above similar stocks in the six months after purchases, about a third in the first month (measured from trade date, not disclosure date).
- loc.51: insider buying predicts better than selling (many reasons to sell, one to buy); officer-directors earn the highest abnormal returns, pure officers the lowest, 10% owners higher than officers. Intensity: piggybacking purchases by at least three insiders within three months averaged just under 2% a month over the past 27 years vs a little over 1% for the S&P 500, before costs; returns inconsistent year to year. → RULE (growth/momentum? or value) B.
## Chunk loc.52–loc.63 (read)
- loc.52: insider buying works better where information asymmetry is high (e.g. biotech vs Exxon); most predictive in transportation, consumer staples (incl. drug companies), technology, financial and business services; least in capital goods, basic materials, energy, consumer cyclical, utilities. Works better in small caps: small caps with intensive insider buying beat the S&P 500 by almost 0.91% a month gross of costs. → conditions on the insider RULE (B).
- loc.53: insider selling mostly uninformative (option compensation, diversification); exceptions: after insiders adopt a written pre-set selling plan the stock underperforms by ~2% over six months; a 2007 study: a CEO's large luxury-home purchase almost always preceded negative performance. Insiders trade like contrarian individuals; institutions trade the opposite way (more momentum). → principle B.
- loc.54: insider-based newsletters did worse than the strategy over the same periods → act quickly. Insider buying clusters in small and distressed companies, so a multiple-insider screen tilts toward small caps and distress; low correlation with momentum. When insiders are net sellers, analyst downgrades bring no unusual returns; downgrades produce abnormal negative returns only when insiders are not selling. → principle B.
- loc.54: international: insider strategies work in Germany, especially the Netherlands; positive in UK, Spain, Italy, Hong Kong, Poland; Spain's 39-day disclosure delay removes the edge; concentrated in small firms.
- loc.55: Brochet (2010), insider purchases 1997–2006: 3-day excess return from the filing date ≈ 0.6% pre-Sarbanes-Oxley (10–40 day filing lag) vs 1.9% after (2-day filing). → supports acting on filing date, B (period given; sample size not stated).
- loc.55: multi-year stretches of underperformance; needs patience through ~3 bad years.
- loc.56–57: market responds mostly within 30 days of the purchase, with more excess return starting ~6 months later; purchases older than five or six months have little predictive ability; long side only; multiple insiders over a window; officer-directors slightly stronger than plain officers; strongest in small caps and distressed companies; volatile → a complement to a core strategy. → RULE: EXIT/expiry when the cluster is older than ~6 months.
- loc.57–58: "Groucho Marx": the IPOs you're offered are the ones to pass on; negative excess returns after IPOs; positive after buybacks, dividend increases, some financings. Companies go public when valuations compel private holders to sell.
- loc.58–59: one study of 1,500+ IPOs: cumulative average return of nearly −30% over the 36 months after the IPO; holds after matching by size and industry; IPOs overvalued by 14–50% on peer matching; a mid-1990s study: over 5 years non-VC-backed IPOs lagged an equal-weight benchmark by 33%, VC-backed by 16%. → RULE: avoid buying IPOs and stocks within 36 months of IPO (value), B.
- loc.60: secondary equity offerings also underperform — "the stocks of companies issuing shares are more likely to be losers" → agrees R-087 (Gibbs dilution) — B.
- loc.60–61: aggregate IPO/equity issuance as a market gauge: heavy issuance → low market returns afterwards; a dearth → strong market for the next ~5 years; equity issuance rising sharply relative to debt issuance = managers see the market as expensive. → principle B (no threshold, market-level). Author's 2011 call that the market would rise → forecast, not carried.
- loc.61–62: IPO studies use first-day close, not offer price; flipping at the offer price is for big clients; small investors get offered the broken IPOs. Results not uniform across periods. → principle.
- loc.62: buybacks: reasons (EPS management, tax-efficient payout, signalling undervaluation — data say undervaluation drives most); rule of thumb: when a company buys back shares, buy too.
- loc.63: self-tender offers: >9% abnormal return in one week (some studies), small caps only; large firms show excess returns before the announcement and none after; rare today. A 1995 study of 1,200+ open-market repurchases in the 1980s: repurchasers usually outperformed over the four years after the announcement; abnormal returns trend in the direction of the first price reaction. → RULE: buyback announcement (value), B (sample and period given, no return figure).
## Chunk loc.64–loc.76 (read)
- loc.64: 2009 follow-up with a larger, more recent sample: buyback excess returns not tied to one period or segment; caution: the company must actually buy the shares, not just announce an open-ended or cancellable program. 3,400+ open-market programs announced in the 1990s: large long-run abnormal returns persisted, especially for value stocks, stronger in small caps. Explanation: buybacks answer analysts who are slow to change their minds (low coverage → stronger). → buyback RULE conditions (B).
- loc.65: undervaluation is the main reason for buybacks (author's reading of the data).
- loc.65: avoid acquirers (winner's curse: the winning bidder usually overpays); an acquisition paid in debt may signal management sees its stock as cheap; a spree paid in stock signals managers see their stock as overvalued. → principle D (no study cited).
- loc.65: dividend initiations get a positive price reaction, eliminations a stronger negative one; long initiators / short eliminators earns significant returns, mostly from the short side. → RULE candidate (dividend eliminated → sell/avoid), B. Check vs R-033.
- loc.66–67: summary: avoid IPOs for three to four years after listing; pass on offer-price allocations unless a very large institution; exception: Dutch-auction IPOs; treat secondary offerings like IPOs — any equity issuance is a negative; equity issuance spiking relative to debt issuance warns the market may cool; small-cap self-tenders → buy the stock and ignore the tender; buy companies that announced substantial buybacks; sell when a company eliminates its dividend; summary idea: when a company is selling its shares, don't buy; when it is buying them back, don't sell (paraphrased).
- loc.67–69: accruals: earnings = cash part + accounting part; trust cash earnings more than accounting earnings; the market fixates on beat/miss and ignores earnings quality.
- loc.69: accrual measure: current net operating assets = (current assets − cash) − (current liabilities − short-term debt − income taxes payable); accruals = this year's minus last year's, often scaled by total assets. High accruals = bad. → RULE inputs (10-K balance sheet), computed in code.
- loc.70–71: studies over four decades: long low-accrual / short high-accrual hedge earned a double-digit annual return gross of costs; high-accrual stocks underperform over the next year; positive in 30 of 38 years; positive in all but one year before 1996, about half the years after; returns declined after ~1998 (likely arbitraged after Sloan's paper; high-institutional-ownership stocks price accruals better). → RULE (value), B.
- loc.71–72: extreme-accrual firms tend to be illiquid and volatile; bonds of high-accrual firms also underperform. Works best where working capital matters (construction, toys, computers, electrical equipment); poorly for drug, mining and energy companies. Helps avoid negative accounting events (excess inventory → restatements).
- loc.73: works globally in US/UK-like legal systems; data timeliness not critical (returns arrive around the next earnings report / negative preannouncements); persists after controlling for cash-flow value multiples (pre-1996). 2008: banks' bad loans showed up as high accruals.
- loc.74–75: excess returns may have started to disappear around 2000; works better on the short side: avoiding high accruals beats seeking low accruals (low accruals flags slow-growth cash businesses); extremes concentrate in thin small caps (hard for institutions to arbitrage). Best use: find stocks to avoid; watch fast-rising inventories (author's experience); high accruals rise naturally with strong growth, so combine with other methods. → RULE as an avoid filter.
- loc.76: no returns without risk; an anomaly = extra return without extra risk; consensus that value beats over long periods, dispute over whether it's extra risk; since 1927 value beat growth in 58% of years, several years by more than 20%. → principle B.

- loc.112–113: G score (Mohanram, Columbia): growth stocks; points when each of eight measures beats the industry median: ROA, operating cash flow ÷ assets, cash flow above earnings (accruals), low 5-year variance of ROA, low 5-year variance of sales growth, R&D ÷ assets, capex ÷ assets, advertising ÷ assets; works by avoiding growth stocks likely to stumble.
